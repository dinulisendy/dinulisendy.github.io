When I tell people I'm simultaneously pursuing a BSc in Transport Management & Logistics Engineering at the University of Moratuwa *and* working through the CIMA (Chartered Institute of Management Accountants) qualification, the reaction is usually some combination of curiosity and concern.

"Isn't that a lot?" Yes. "Isn't CIMA for accountants?" Technically, no — and that's the whole point.

## Why CIMA for an Engineering Student?

Management consulting, particularly in infrastructure and logistics, sits at the intersection of technical feasibility and financial viability. A project can be engineered brilliantly and still fail because the business case doesn't hold, the cost model is wrong, or the risk-adjusted return doesn't meet the client's threshold.

I observed this pattern early in my studies: engineering teams often produce technically excellent work that struggles to gain organisational buy-in because it can't be communicated in the language that executives and boards use — which is fundamentally a financial language.

CIMA exists to build exactly that bridge. The Management Level qualification covers:

- **Management Accounting** — cost and budget analysis, variance analysis, pricing decisions
- **Financial Reporting** — understanding the financial statements that drive business decisions
- **Management Information Systems** — data, analytics, and performance management frameworks
- **Business Strategy** — strategic analysis tools (Porter's Five Forces, SWOT, PESTEL) in a management accounting context
- **Risk Management** — identifying, quantifying, and managing operational and financial risk

For someone building a career in transport consulting, this curriculum reads like a checklist of things I actually need to know.

## The Reality of Parallel Study

I won't romanticise it. Managing semester coursework, laboratory sessions, and CIMA study simultaneously requires very deliberate time management. A few things that have helped:

**Structured weekly blocks, not daily cramming.** I allocate specific time blocks to CIMA study that don't move — treating them like scheduled lectures rather than optional revision sessions.

**Finding the cross-over material.** The Management Accounting content has significant overlap with the quantitative methods and cost analysis components of my engineering programme. Studying both simultaneously actually creates reinforcement — the same concepts land more solidly when approached from two different frameworks.

**McKinsey Forward as a catalyst.** The McKinsey Forward Programme (which I completed in June 2026) included structured modules on problem-solving, communication, and personal effectiveness. It gave me a vocabulary for consulting thinking that made the CIMA strategy modules click much more clearly.

## What Hybrid Skillsets Actually Unlock

The honest answer is that I don't yet know the full value of this combination — I'm still building it. But I have a working hypothesis: the most interesting roles in infrastructure consulting over the next decade will go to people who can *understand* a load model and *read* a P&L.

Technical specialists are essential. Financial specialists are essential. But the translation layer — someone who can sit in a client workshop and follow both the engineering dialogue and the CFO's concerns about capital allocation — is where value gets created and captured.

That's the role I'm building toward.

## Completing CIMA — The Road Ahead

I completed the Management Level in 2025. The Strategic Level (expected August 2026) covers strategic management accounting, strategic financial management, and the case study examination — a three-hour simulated business scenario that tests integrated application across all pillars.

After that: the CIMA Fellowship designation requires three years of relevant work experience. Which means the clock starts when I start working — and the sooner I start building professional experience, the sooner I can close the loop on the full qualification.

If you're an engineering student wondering whether professional accounting qualifications are worth the investment of time: they are, but only if you're clear about *why* you're pursuing them. For me, the reason is simple — I want to add value at the decision-making level, not just the technical analysis level. CIMA is the credential that signals that capability.
